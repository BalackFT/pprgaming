**Europe just put a target on loot boxes.** On Wednesday the European Commission [adopted its proposal for a Digital Fairness Act](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/consumer-protection-law/digital-fairness-act_en), a rewrite of EU consumer law that the Commission calls the biggest upgrade in over 20 years. Most of it is about dark patterns, subscription traps and influencer ads, but a big chunk lands squarely on how games make money.

**What it would change in games.** Going by the [draft regulation](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/0575d59f-554f-4a9e-b54b-e6b717175809_en) and [iGaming Business's breakdown](https://igamingbusiness.com/legal-compliance/eu-digital-fairness-act-tighten-rules-loot-boxes-gambling-influencer-marketing/):

- **Loot box odds up front.** Any paid "variable reward system" (the draft names loot boxes, wheels of fortune and card packs) would have to show what's inside and the odds of getting each item.
- **An off switch for everyone.** Players of any age would get tools to turn paid random rewards off.
- **Real prices on fake money.** Items bought with paid virtual currency would have to show their real-money value "in a prominent, clear and comprehensible manner."
- **Kids get walled off.** In products aimed at minors, paid loot boxes couldn't be offered, virtual currency would be off by default until a parent approves it, and the Commission says [pay-to-win and pay-to-progress would be banned for minors](https://cyprus.representation.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-strengthens-consumer-protection-laws-making-them-fit-digital-age-2026-11-11_en).

The draft treats a game as aimed at minors if its age rating, PEGI included, says it's suitable for under-18s. That's most games on the shelf.

**The industry is not happy.** Video Games Europe and the European Games Developer Federation put out a [joint statement](https://videogameseurope.eu/news/joint-statement-from-the-european-games-developer-federation-egdf-and-video-games-europe-vge-on-the-digital-fairness-act) headlined "The DFA is game over for the European games industry," calling the proposal "unworkable, unjustified and disproportionate." Their biggest complaints: the pay-to-win ban "lacks clear definitions" and "could cover almost any purchase in a game," with fines of up to 6% of global turnover hanging over it, and treating every sub-18 rating as "directed at children" could push publishers to shut teens out. They point to PEGI's new in-game purchase ratings and console parental controls as protections that already work, [PocketGamer.biz reports](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/the-dfa-is-game-over-for-the-european-games-industry/).

**Nothing changes tomorrow.** This is still a proposal. The European Parliament and the Council have to negotiate and adopt it, and if it passes as written it would apply 12 months after entering into force, [per iGaming Business](https://igamingbusiness.com/legal-compliance/eu-digital-fairness-act-tighten-rules-loot-boxes-gambling-influencer-marketing/).

## PPR take

For players, most of this sounds good on paper. Seeing drop rates before you open your wallet and seeing what a skin really costs in euros instead of "1,200 gems" are things a lot of us have wanted for years, and an opt-out for loot boxes costs nobody anything. The part to watch is the definitions. If "pay-to-progress" ends up meaning any XP boost or time-saver, and if a PEGI 16 rating counts as "aimed at children," publishers won't rebuild their games for Europe. They'll age-gate, strip features, or keep separate EU versions, and teens will feel that first. The industry's warning is self-interested, sure, but it's not wrong that vague rules backed by 6% fines lead to cautious, worse games. The good news is there's a long negotiation ahead, and the end result could look very different. And because Europe is too big a market to ignore, whatever survives will probably shape how live-service games sell to the rest of us too.
